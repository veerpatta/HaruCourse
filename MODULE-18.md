### Start here: in everyday words

An independent project brings the research, design, testing and decision trail together around one honest problem. In this lesson, your first small result is: Three candidate domains you have not used, each with somebody you know and a problem you have heard them complain about.

**Words you will use**

- **A new domain:** Different from your earlier projects, so the portfolio shows range rather than the same setting three times.
- **The access gate:** Three agreements before committing. It is the decision most independent projects get wrong, and it is made first here for that reason.

### See the decision being made

Section: practice-plan. Stable action: step-2-demo.

Made-up example. Choosing a third project, and choosing the interesting one.

**What I chose:** A domain I found genuinely interesting, where I knew nobody, on a problem I had read about rather than heard about.

**What I told myself:** That I would find participants once the project was under way. Everybody says this, and it is the sentence that produces invented findings a month later.

**What happened over three weeks:** Eleven messages, two replies, no agreements. The project had not started and I had spent a fifth of my appetite on recruitment.

**What I did:** Went back to the list and took the third domain: a repair shop whose owner had complained about the same thing twice, in front of me, unprompted.

**How long access took there:** One conversation. He agreed to two sessions and introduced me to two customers, because he already wanted the problem solved.

**Wrong turn:** The wrong turn is choosing by interest and treating access as a later step. Access is the first decision, and the project that stalls is not the one with a boring domain; it is the one where nobody has said yes.

**Trade-off:** Choosing for access means working on somebody else’s problem in a setting you would not have picked, and a repair shop is less appealing to write up than the domain I wanted.

**Unknown:** Still unknown: whether the interesting domain would have worked eventually. Three weeks of silence is what I had, and the appetite was four.

### See the decision being made

Section: practice. Stable action: step-5-demo.

Made-up example. Writing an abandonment condition, and writing one that could never be met.

**What I wrote:** “I will stop if the project becomes unviable.” It sounded prudent and it committed me to nothing.

**What happened in week three:** The owner was away, then busy, then away again. Nothing was unviable; it was simply not happening, and each week looked survivable on its own.

**How long that continued:** Five weeks, on a four-week appetite, with two sessions completed. At no point was there a moment where stopping was obviously right.

**What a real condition would have done:** Two consecutive weeks without a session means stop. It would have triggered in week four, with a decision rather than a drift.

**What stopping would have produced:** An access record, a research plan, two sessions and a written account of why a domain with one gatekeeper is fragile. That is a portfolio item; five weeks of drift is not.

**Wrong turn:** The wrong turn is writing a condition about viability, because it sounds like judgement and nothing triggers it. A project rarely becomes unviable; it becomes slower, week by week, with each week defensible.

**Trade-off:** A countable condition will sometimes stop a project that would have recovered in week five, and the alternative is a project that never ends and never finishes.

**Unknown:** Still unknown: whether this project would have recovered. It might have, and the appetite was four weeks and the condition is what makes that mean something.

### Scope by cutting features

Section: practice-plan. Stable action: step-3-brief.

Everything the ideal solution would include, and what survives with states and accessibility intact.

- List everything the ideal solution would include.
- Cut features until it fits, keeping states and accessibility intact.

**Start here:** List the ideal version first, then remove whole features rather than trimming each.

**Enough:** Nothing was cut from the quality of what remains.

**Cutting by feature:** Removing whole things rather than doing everything less well. It keeps what remains defensible.

**Cutting by quality:** Keeping every feature and dropping the states, the failure paths and the accessibility work. It is what happens by default when nobody decides.


### See the decision being made

Section: practice-plan. Stable action: step-3-demo.

Made-up example. Scoping a repair-shop status page to three weeks, and keeping everything.

**What I wanted to build:** A status page, notifications when the status changes, a quote record, and a simple account so people could see their history. Four features, all sensible.

**How I made it fit:** Kept all four and did each more simply. No error states, one happy path each, accessibility left until the end.

**What that produced at the end of three weeks:** Four half-features, none of which survived a person using it. The status page had no stale-data state and the notifications had no failure case.

**What cutting by feature would have produced:** One status page, with its eleven exception rows, a keyboard pass and honest wording. Something that works.

**What the not-building list said:** Notifications: back if the status page is used and people still ring. Quote records: a different problem, deferred. Accounts: refused outright, because the page can be reached by job number and an account is a barrier.

**Wrong turn:** The wrong turn is keeping every feature and doing each less well, because cutting a feature feels like failing and doing things simply feels like discipline. What gets dropped is always the states and the accessibility, because they are the parts still unfinished at the end.

**Trade-off:** One feature is a thinner-sounding project, and somebody looking at the portfolio will see less on the page.

**Unknown:** Still unknown: whether notifications are the right second thing. The return condition is written and nobody has tested it.

### See the decision being made

Section: practice. Stable action: step-5-demo.

Made-up example. Checking a problem frame with the person who described it, and checking it by explaining.

**What I did:** Went through the whole project with the shop owner: the research, the findings, the counts, and then the frame at the end.

**What he said:** That it sounded right. Twenty minutes of context, and by the time the sentence arrived he was agreeing with a project rather than reading a statement.

**What the second attempt was:** One message, with the sentence and nothing else, asking whether that is what he meant.

**What came back:** “Sort of. It is not the waiting that annoys people, it is being told twice that it will be ready tomorrow.” One line, and it changed the wording of every state on the page.

**Why the message worked and the meeting did not:** A sentence on its own can be disagreed with. A sentence at the end of your explanation is a conclusion he has just been walked to.

**Wrong turn:** The wrong turn is presenting the frame in context, because context is what makes it make sense. It also makes disagreement much harder, and the correction is the entire value of the step.

**Trade-off:** A bare sentence risks being misread, and you may get a correction to something you did not mean rather than to what you did.

**Unknown:** Still unknown: whether being told twice is the general case or his strongest memory. One person corrected one thing, and it is written as his correction rather than as a finding.

- One problem is enough. A four-week project that addresses three problems addresses none of them well, and the portfolio value comes from depth rather than coverage. Choosing which problem is a decision to record, with the reason, because a reviewer will ask.

<summary>Reading, video and deeper explanation</summary>

- Chronological order is the honest order and the easiest to check. A record arranged to build a narrative is already a case study, and case studies written before the record exists tend to acquire claims the record cannot support.
- The claim-to-artefact check is mechanical and worth doing: read each sentence that asserts something, and find the file that supports it. Anything unsupported either gets its artefact attached, gets weakened to what the evidence shows, or gets deleted.
- The failures carry weight. The recruitment that did not work, the repair that did not fix the problem, the measurement confounded by a public holiday — these are what make the successes believable, and removing them removes the credibility of everything left.
- Anonymity and durability are both practical. People appear by role, consent records stay out of anything shareable, and the whole record lives in at least two places, because losing three months of work to a failed drive is a common and entirely avoidable ending.
