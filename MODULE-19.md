## Lesson 1: What a portfolio has to do in ninety seconds

Stable ID: m19-l01-v1. Core. Areas 17. Optional effort ~110 min.

**Objective.** Establish what a reviewer is trying to find out, and write the three claims your portfolio has to support.

**Bring forward.** Your three project records.

A portfolio built without knowing what question it answers becomes a gallery, and a gallery answers nothing.

### Learn

The reviewer's question is narrow: could this person be handed a real problem and make it better without constant supervision? Everything in the portfolio either helps answer that or takes up space. Knowing the question is what lets you cut.

The first pass is a skim of a minute or two, and it decides whether there is a second pass. That means the top of each case study has to carry the problem, your role and the outcome, because that is all a skim will reach.

Writing your three claims first inverts the usual order and prevents the common failure, which is assembling everything you have and hoping a story emerges. Three claims — for example that you can turn research into a decision, that you repair work when testing shows it failing, and that you state limits — give every later choice a test.

Craft still matters, but craft alone reads as decoration in this discipline, because a screen cannot show whether the problem was worth solving. Your visual background is an advantage only when it sits alongside the reasoning.

**Common misconception.** “The portfolio should show everything I have done.” It should support three claims. Work that supports none of them makes the portfolio longer and the claims weaker.

### Worked example

Three claims chosen: I turn research into a decision that can be traced; I test and repair rather than defending the first version; I state what my work does not establish. Each was written with the project that best supports it and the specific artefact behind it — the synthesis and framing page for the first, the repair-and-re-test record for the second, and the limitations page for the third. A fourth candidate claim, about building interfaces from a system, was dropped for now because the evidence was thinner and three is what a skim can hold.

### Practice and pause points

- Write the reviewer's question (~20 min): Write, in one sentence, what a reviewer is deciding about you. Write what would make them stop reading.
- Choose three claims (~30 min): List candidate claims your three projects could support. Choose three, preferring the ones with the strongest artefacts.
- Attach the evidence (~25 min): For each claim, name the project and the specific artefact. Reject any claim whose artefact you cannot point to.
- Identify the surplus (~20 min): List work that supports none of the three claims. Mark it as excluded rather than deleting it.
- Sanity-check the skim (~15 min): Ask whether a ninety-second skim would reach all three claims. Save the claims and their evidence.

Pause after any step; save the artifact and next action.

**Free tool path.** Writing only. No portfolio tool, template or subscription is needed at this stage, and choosing one now would be premature.

### Output

A written statement of what a reviewer is deciding; Three claims your portfolio will support; The project and artefact behind each claim; A list of work that supports none of them

### Checks

- What is the reviewer actually deciding? Answer: Whether you could be handed a real problem and improve it without constant supervision. Everything else in the portfolio is in service of that.
- Why write the claims before the case studies? Answer: Because otherwise you assemble everything you have and hope a story emerges. Claims first give every later choice a test to pass.
- Why exclude work rather than include it? Answer: Because work supporting none of the claims lengthens the portfolio and dilutes the claims a skim can actually reach.

### Rubric and remediation

**The reviewer's question is stated**

Adequate evidence: One sentence naming what a reviewer is deciding.

- 0 — Not stated.
- 1 — Stated vaguely as “showing my work”.
- 2 — Stated as a decision about capability.
- 3 — As adequate, and it names what would stop them reading.

If below 2: Write what a person hiring would need to believe to take the risk. Show at recheck: The statement.

**Three claims are chosen deliberately**

Adequate evidence: Exactly three claims, chosen from a longer list.

- 0 — No claims, or an undifferentiated list.
- 1 — Three claims without alternatives considered.
- 2 — Three chosen from candidates.
- 3 — As adequate, and the rejected claims are recorded with reasons.

If below 2: List every claim your projects could support and choose the best-evidenced three. Show at recheck: The claims list.

**Each claim names a specific artefact**

Adequate evidence: A project and a named artefact behind each claim.

- 0 — Claims with no evidence attached.
- 1 — Projects named, artefacts vague.
- 2 — Specific artefacts named.
- 3 — As adequate, and at least one candidate claim was dropped for weak evidence.

If below 2: For each claim, open the record and name the file that supports it. Show at recheck: The evidence map.

**Surplus work is identified and excluded**

Adequate evidence: A list of work supporting none of the claims.

- 0 — Everything kept.
- 1 — Some work questioned.
- 2 — Surplus identified and excluded.
- 3 — As adequate, and it is kept aside rather than deleted.

If below 2: Test each piece of work against the three claims and set aside what fails. Show at recheck: The exclusion list.

### Portfolio contribution

The claims and their evidence map is the plan for the whole module; every later lesson is checked against it.

## Lesson 2: Auditing what you actually kept

Stable ID: m19-l02-v1. Core. Areas 17. Optional effort ~120 min.

**Objective.** Audit all three project records against the three claims, and record precisely which evidence exists, which is thin and which is missing.

**Bring forward.** Your three claims and three project records.

You can only write case studies from what you kept, and finding the gaps now is cheaper than discovering them mid-sentence.

### Learn

Memory is generous about your own work. The audit has to be done against the files: open each folder, list what is there, and mark what each item can support. The difference between what you remember doing and what you can show is usually larger than expected and always worth knowing.

Three states are enough. Strong means an artefact a reviewer could read and believe; thin means it exists but is partial or undated; missing means there is nothing. Anything thin or missing constrains what the case study can say.

The temptation at this point is to redraw a flow, rewrite notes more neatly or produce the synthesis you meant to do. Making a missing artefact now and presenting it as part of the project is fabrication, and it is the specific dishonesty this discipline punishes hardest because it is so easy to check.

Some gaps are genuinely closable. If the third project's participant is still reachable, a short follow-up conversation is real new evidence, dated today, and can be presented as exactly that. Closing a gap honestly and closing it by invention look similar on the page and are entirely different acts.

**Common misconception.** “I will tidy up the artefacts before showing them.” Tidying presentation is fine; producing a document that never existed and dating it to the project is not. The line is whether the evidence existed when you claim it did.

### Worked example

The audit covered three projects and produced a table of twenty-six artefacts. Strong: two synthesis pages, one repair-and-re-test record, one limitations page, one handover package. Thin: the first project's session notes, which were written two days later and say so; the second project's flow, which is undated. Missing: any accessibility evidence from projects one and two, and any before-measurement from project two. Two gaps were closable — a keyboard pass on the second project's prototype, run today and labelled with today's date, and a short follow-up with the shop owner. The rest were recorded as permanent limits.

### Practice and pause points

- Inventory (~30 min): Open each project folder and list what exists. Do not work from memory.
- Mark the strength (~25 min): Mark each item strong, thin or missing. Note why anything is thin, including undated or late artefacts.
- Test against the claims (~25 min): For each of your three claims, list the strong evidence available. Flag any claim that rests only on thin evidence.
- Separate closable gaps (~20 min): Identify gaps you could close honestly this week. Plan each one and note that it will carry today's date.
- Record the permanent gaps (~20 min): List gaps that cannot be closed. Write how each will be stated in the case study.

Pause after any step; save the artifact and next action.

**Free tool path.** A table in a text file and the project folders.

### Output

An audit table of every artefact across three projects; Each item marked strong, thin or missing; A list of closable gaps with dates; A list of permanent gaps to be stated as limits

### Checks

- Why audit the files rather than your memory? Answer: Because memory is generous about your own work, and the gap between what you did and what you can show is usually larger than expected.
- What separates closing a gap from fabricating one? Answer: Whether the evidence existed when you say it did. New work dated today is honest; a document produced now and presented as contemporaneous is not.
- What happens to a claim resting on thin evidence? Answer: It gets weakened to what the evidence supports, replaced by a better-evidenced claim, or supported by honestly dated new work.

### Rubric and remediation

**The audit is drawn from the files**

Adequate evidence: An inventory matching what is actually in the folders.

- 0 — Written from memory.
- 1 — Partly checked.
- 2 — Every item verified in the folder.
- 3 — As adequate, and items you thought existed but do not are listed.

If below 2: Open every folder and list its contents item by item. Show at recheck: The inventory.

**Every artefact is marked strong, thin or missing**

Adequate evidence: A three-state mark on each item with reasons for thin ones.

- 0 — Unmarked.
- 1 — Marked without reasons.
- 2 — Marked with reasons for thin items.
- 3 — As adequate, and lateness or missing dates are recorded.

If below 2: Mark each item and say why the thin ones are thin. Show at recheck: The audit table.

**Claims are tested against available evidence**

Adequate evidence: Each claim with its strong evidence listed.

- 0 — Claims untested.
- 1 — Tested informally.
- 2 — Each claim mapped to strong evidence.
- 3 — As adequate, and any claim resting on thin evidence is flagged.

If below 2: Go claim by claim and list only the strong items that support it. Show at recheck: The claim mapping.

**Closable and permanent gaps are separated**

Adequate evidence: Two lists, with dates planned for the closable ones.

- 0 — Gaps not identified.
- 1 — One undifferentiated list.
- 2 — Separated with plans and dates.
- 3 — As adequate, and no closable gap will be presented as contemporaneous.

If below 2: Split the gaps by whether you can honestly close them this week. Show at recheck: The gap lists.

### Portfolio contribution

The audit table is the boundary of what the portfolio can honestly claim, and keeping it makes every later sentence checkable.

## Lesson 3: Deciding what each case study is for

Stable ID: m19-l03-v1. Core. Areas 17. Optional effort ~110 min.

**Objective.** Assign each of the three projects a single job in the portfolio, and write the one-sentence promise each case study makes.

**Bring forward.** Your claims and your audit table.

Three case studies that all say the same thing are one case study repeated, and reviewers notice.

### Learn

One job per case study is the discipline that makes three feel like a portfolio rather than an archive. If project one has the best research trail and project three has the only measurement, those are their jobs, and everything in each is selected to serve it.

The promise sentence is what the case study will demonstrate, written so it could be checked. “Shows that I can turn five conversations into a framed problem and a decision” is checkable; “shows my UX process” is not.

Order matters because the skim is sequential and attention decays. The strongest case study goes first even if it is not the most recent, and the weakest may not belong in the portfolio at all.

Equal length is a mistake beginners make out of fairness. A project with a thin record deserves a shorter, more focused case study, and the shortness itself reads as judgement rather than as a gap.

**Common misconception.** “Each case study should show my whole process.” Then all three show the same thing and the reader learns one fact about you three times. Give each a job the others do not do.

### Worked example

Project three took the research-to-decision job because its synthesis and framing were strongest and it had the only measurement. Project two took the iteration job, built entirely around a repair that failed on the first attempt and worked on the second. Project one took the craft-and-constraint job, kept short because its records were thin, focused on the interface work and the accessibility repair, and explicit that its research was a single conversation. Order: three, two, one. Lengths planned at roughly 900, 700 and 400 words.

### Practice and pause points

- Match jobs to evidence (~25 min): For each project, name its strongest evidence from the audit. Assign a job no other case study will do.
- Write the promises (~25 min): Write one checkable sentence per case study. Reject any sentence a reader could not test.
- Order them (~20 min): Put the strongest first regardless of date. Decide whether the weakest belongs at all.
- Plan lengths (~20 min): Assign a length proportional to the evidence. Keep the thinnest project deliberately short.
- Check for repetition (~20 min): Read the three promises together. If two overlap, change one job or drop a case study.

Pause after any step; save the artifact and next action.

**Free tool path.** Writing only.

### Output

One job assigned to each case study; A checkable promise sentence for each; An order, with the strongest first; A planned length for each, not necessarily equal

### Checks

- What makes a promise sentence usable? Answer: That a reader could check it against the case study. “Shows my process” cannot be checked; a specific demonstration can.
- Why not equal lengths? Answer: Because length should follow evidence. A short case study on a thin project reads as judgement; a padded one reads as padding.
- Why does the strongest go first? Answer: Because attention decays through a portfolio, and the first case study decides whether there is a second.

### Rubric and remediation

**Each case study has a distinct job**

Adequate evidence: Three jobs, none duplicated.

- 0 — All three show the same thing.
- 1 — Two overlap.
- 2 — Three distinct jobs.
- 3 — As adequate, and each job matches that project's strongest evidence.

If below 2: Compare the three jobs and reassign until none repeats another. Show at recheck: The job assignments.

**Promises are single checkable sentences**

Adequate evidence: One testable sentence per case study.

- 0 — Vague statements of process.
- 1 — Specific but untestable.
- 2 — Checkable against the case study.
- 3 — As adequate, and each promise names the artefact that proves it.

If below 2: Rewrite each promise as something a reader could verify. Show at recheck: The promises.

**The order puts the strongest first**

Adequate evidence: A stated order justified by strength, not date.

- 0 — Chronological by default.
- 1 — Ordered without reasoning.
- 2 — Strongest first with reasoning.
- 3 — As adequate, and the weakest was considered for removal.

If below 2: Rank by evidence strength and reorder. Show at recheck: The order.

**Lengths follow the evidence**

Adequate evidence: Planned lengths proportional to what each project can show.

- 0 — Equal lengths by default.
- 1 — Unequal without reasoning.
- 2 — Proportional to evidence.
- 3 — As adequate, and the shortest is short on purpose.

If below 2: Cut the length of any case study whose record is thin. Show at recheck: The length plan.

### Portfolio contribution

The three promises are the contract each case study has to keep, and the last lesson checks them again before publishing.

## Lesson 4: The case study skeleton

Stable ID: m19-l04-v1. Core. Areas 17. Optional effort ~130 min.

**Objective.** Write the first case study to a fixed structure — problem, evidence, decision, iteration, limits — with every claim traced to an artefact.

**Bring forward.** Your strongest project and its promise sentence.

A structure stops the case study becoming a chronology, which is the form most junior portfolios take and the one reviewers skip.

### Learn

The five sections work because they answer the reviewer's questions in order: what was wrong, how do you know, what did you do about it, what happened when you tested it, and what does this not prove. A chronology answers none of them and asks the reader to do the work.

Leading with problem and outcome respects the skim. The reader who stops after two paragraphs should still know what the project was and what came of it; the reader who continues gets the reasoning.

Tracing claims to artefacts is the mechanical check from m18 applied to prose. Each assertion either has a file behind it or gets weakened. Doing it while writing is far easier than retrofitting it later.

Depth over breadth: one decision explained properly — the options, the evidence, the trade-off, the thing you gave up — teaches a reviewer more than eight decisions listed. It is also the part they will ask about in an interview, so choose one you can defend.

**Common misconception.** “The case study should follow the order I did the work in.” The work's order was messy and full of dead ends. The reader needs the reasoning, arranged for understanding, with the dead ends kept where they are instructive.

### Worked example

The first case study runs: problem, in the shop owner's words with the count of interrupted calls; evidence, five participants, what was found, what contradicted, and the exclusions; decision, one framed problem and the not-building list, with the appetite; iteration, the wording repair that failed for one of two re-testers and what that meant; limits, the study and design limits page in short form. Nineteen claims, each with a named artefact. One claim about the owner's time saved was cut because nothing measured it.

### Practice and pause points

- Draft the problem (~25 min): Write the problem in the person's words, with a count if you have one. State the outcome in the same opening.
- Write the evidence section (~30 min): Give participant counts, the key findings and one contradiction. Include the exclusions.
- Write one decision in depth (~30 min): Give the options, the evidence, the trade-off and what you gave up. Choose a decision you can defend under questioning.
- Write iteration and limits (~25 min): Show a change, its prediction and the re-test result. Summarise the study and design limits.
- Trace the claims (~20 min): List every assertion and name its artefact. Weaken or cut anything unsupported.

Pause after any step; save the artifact and next action.

**Free tool path.** A Markdown file. No portfolio platform is required yet.

### Output

One case study in five sections; Problem and outcome in the first two paragraphs; One decision explained in depth; A claim-to-artefact trace for the whole piece

### Checks

- Why not tell it in the order it happened? Answer: Because the work's order was messy. The reader needs reasoning arranged for understanding, with dead ends kept only where they teach something.
- Why one decision rather than all of them? Answer: Because depth shows judgement and breadth shows activity. It is also what an interviewer will ask about, so it should be one you can defend.
- What happens to an unsupported claim? Answer: It is weakened to what the artefact shows or cut. Doing this while writing is much cheaper than retrofitting it.

### Rubric and remediation

**The five sections are present and in order**

Adequate evidence: Problem, evidence, decision, iteration and limits.

- 0 — A chronology.
- 1 — Some sections present.
- 2 — All five, in order.
- 3 — As adequate, and each section could be read alone.

If below 2: Restructure the draft into the five sections. Show at recheck: The structure.

**Problem and outcome appear in the opening**

Adequate evidence: Both within the first two paragraphs.

- 0 — Buried after process description.
- 1 — Problem early, outcome late.
- 2 — Both in the opening.
- 3 — As adequate, and a reader stopping there would know what happened.

If below 2: Move the outcome up and cut the process preamble. Show at recheck: The opening.

**One decision is explained in depth**

Adequate evidence: Options, evidence, trade-off and what was given up.

- 0 — Decisions listed.
- 1 — One decision described without alternatives.
- 2 — One decision with options and trade-off.
- 3 — As adequate, and it names what was given up and why that was acceptable.

If below 2: Pick your strongest decision and write its alternatives and cost. Show at recheck: The decision section.

**Every claim traces to an artefact**

Adequate evidence: A claim list with artefacts named.

- 0 — No trace performed.
- 1 — Traced informally.
- 2 — Every claim traced.
- 3 — As adequate, and at least one claim was cut for lack of evidence.

If below 2: List the assertions and attach, weaken or cut each one. Show at recheck: The claim trace.

### Portfolio contribution

This is the first of the three case studies and the template for the other two.

## Lesson 5: Stating your role without inflating it

Stable ID: m19-l05-v1. Core. Areas 17. Optional effort ~100 min.

**Objective.** Write an accurate role statement for each project, distinguishing self-directed practice from paid or team work, and name every person who contributed.

**Bring forward.** Your three projects and their records.

Role inflation is the most commonly detected dishonesty in portfolios, and the easiest to avoid.

### Learn

The category comes first: paid client work, employment, course exercise or self-directed practice. Reviewers ask this within the first two questions, and having it already on the page converts a suspicious question into a settled fact.

Naming what others did protects you. If the shop owner updated the page, if a friend who writes code helped with the timing logic, if a reviewer's critique produced a change, say so. It costs you nothing and its absence, discovered later, costs everything.

The grammatical tell is “we”. Solo projects written in the first person plural read as team projects, and reviewers have learned to probe it. If you were alone, write “I”; if there were two of you, say so and say who did what.

Self-directed work is not a lesser category. A project run alone, with real participants and honest limits, demonstrates more initiative than a team project where your contribution was two screens. The only thing that damages it is presenting it as something else.

**Common misconception.** “Saying it was a course project will make it count for less.” Saying nothing and being asked is far worse. A labelled self-directed project with real evidence beats an ambiguously framed one every time.

### Worked example

Project three: self-directed practice, unpaid, conducted with the owner's agreement. I did the research, synthesis, design, build and testing. The owner arranged access to two customers and updated the page during the trial. A friend who writes software reviewed the HTML and corrected a date-handling bug — named with their permission. Two reviewers critiqued the work; one comment changed the wording. First person singular throughout, checked by searching the draft for “we”, which appeared four times and was wrong each time.

### Practice and pause points

- Categorise (~20 min): Label each project paid, employment, course or self-directed. Put the label near the top of the case study.
- Split the work (~25 min): List what you did and what anyone else did. Include participants who arranged access.
- Credit contributors (~20 min): Ask permission before naming anyone. Use a role if permission is not given.
- Search for false plurals (~15 min): Search the drafts for “we”, “our” and “the team”. Correct each one that has no team behind it.
- Read it as a sceptic (~20 min): Read each role statement as someone looking for exaggeration. Weaken anything that would not survive a follow-up question.

Pause after any step; save the artifact and next action.

**Free tool path.** Writing, plus a search of your own draft.

### Output

A category for each project: paid, course or self-directed; A statement of what you did and what others did; Contributors credited with permission; A draft checked for false plurals

### Checks

- Why label the project category? Answer: Because a reviewer will ask within two questions, and having it on the page turns a suspicious question into a settled fact.
- What is wrong with “we” on a solo project? Answer: It implies a team that did not exist. Reviewers probe it, and an inflated role discovered late discredits everything else.
- Does self-directed work count for less? Answer: No. Run properly with real participants and honest limits it shows initiative. Only misrepresenting it damages the work.

### Rubric and remediation

**Each project's category is stated near the top**

Adequate evidence: A visible label: paid, employment, course or self-directed.

- 0 — Unstated.
- 1 — Stated somewhere in the body.
- 2 — Stated near the top of each.
- 3 — As adequate, and it is stated without apology.

If below 2: Add the label to the opening of each case study. Show at recheck: The labels.

**Your contribution and others' are separated**

Adequate evidence: An explicit split of who did what.

- 0 — Undifferentiated.
- 1 — Your work stated, others' implied.
- 2 — Both stated explicitly.
- 3 — As adequate, and it includes people who arranged access.

If below 2: List every person who touched the project and what they did. Show at recheck: The contribution split.

**Contributors are credited with permission**

Adequate evidence: Names used only with permission, roles otherwise.

- 0 — Uncredited, or named without asking.
- 1 — Credited inconsistently.
- 2 — Credited with permission or by role.
- 3 — As adequate, and participants stay anonymous by role.

If below 2: Ask before naming anyone; use a role where permission is absent. Show at recheck: The credits.

**The drafts contain no false plurals**

Adequate evidence: A search showing “we” only where a team existed.

- 0 — Plural throughout a solo project.
- 1 — Mixed.
- 2 — Corrected everywhere.
- 3 — As adequate, and the search was actually run on the files.

If below 2: Search each draft and correct every unsupported plural. Show at recheck: The drafts.

### Portfolio contribution

An accurate role statement is what makes every other claim in the case study believable.

## Lesson 6: Showing research without exposing people

Stable ID: m19-l06-v1. Core. Areas 17. Optional effort ~110 min.

**Objective.** Prepare the research material for public display: anonymised, consented for this use, and still specific enough to be evidence.

**Bring forward.** Your research records and consent material.

A portfolio is publication, and the consent you took for research does not automatically cover it.

### Learn

Consent to take part in research is not consent to appear in a public portfolio, and the distinction is real rather than pedantic. Read what you told participants. If it said the notes would be used for the project, publishing a photograph of their shop goes beyond it, and the remedy is to ask them now.

Anonymising well means removing what identifies while keeping what evidences. “Customer B, collecting a repaired phone, said she had already rung twice” is anonymous and specific. “A user expressed frustration” is anonymous and useless.

Images are the common failure. A photograph of a workshop shows other people's property and possibly other people; a screenshot of a real record shows real names and numbers. Recreate with clearly synthetic data, label it as recreated, and say what was changed.

Where consent does not cover publication and cannot be obtained, describe the evidence rather than showing it. “Five sessions, notes retained privately” is honest and still supports a claim; publishing without cover is a breach whatever it adds.

**Common misconception.** “It is anonymous, so it is fine.” Anonymity is one requirement. Consent for this use is another, and a photograph of an identifiable place fails both regardless of whether a name appears.

### Worked example

The consent script said notes would be used for a personal learning project and could be shown in a portfolio, which covered publication for two projects but not the first, where the wording stopped at the project. For the first project the research is described rather than shown. Participants appear as owner, customer A, B and C. Two quotations were trimmed to remove a street name and a phone model that identified an individual. The status page screenshot was recreated with an invented job number and the label “recreated with synthetic data; timings and job number are invented, layout unchanged”.

### Practice and pause points

- Read your consent (~25 min): Read what you actually told each participant. Mark which projects are covered for publication.
- Anonymise the quotations (~25 min): Replace names with roles. Remove identifying details while keeping the specifics.
- Handle the images (~25 min): Recreate anything showing real data or premises. Label recreations and say what was changed.
- Decide what to describe (~20 min): Where consent falls short, describe instead of showing. Write the sentence that does the describing.
- Ask where you can (~15 min): Where a participant is reachable, ask for publication consent. Record the answer and the date.

Pause after any step; save the artifact and next action.

**Free tool path.** Text editing and any image editor you already have. Recreation with synthetic data needs no new tool.

### Output

A check of what your consent actually permitted; Anonymised quotations that stay specific; Recreated or blurred images, labelled; A described-not-shown decision where consent falls short

### Checks

- Does research consent cover a portfolio? Answer: Not automatically. Read what you told participants; if it stopped at the project, publication needs a fresh ask or a description instead.
- What does good anonymisation preserve? Answer: The specifics that make it evidence — counts, actions, the concrete situation — while removing what identifies the person.
- Why recreate a screenshot? Answer: Because a real one carries real names and numbers. A recreation with synthetic data, labelled as such, shows the design without exposing anyone.

### Rubric and remediation

**Consent coverage is checked per project**

Adequate evidence: A per-project note of what consent permitted.

- 0 — Not checked.
- 1 — Assumed from memory.
- 2 — Checked against the actual wording.
- 3 — As adequate, and fresh consent was sought where possible.

If below 2: Read the consent wording for each project and record what it covers. Show at recheck: The coverage note.

**Quotations are anonymous and still specific**

Adequate evidence: Role-labelled quotations retaining concrete detail.

- 0 — Names or identifying detail present.
- 1 — Anonymous but vague.
- 2 — Anonymous and specific.
- 3 — As adequate, and what was trimmed is noted.

If below 2: Trim identifying detail without removing the concrete situation. Show at recheck: The quotations.

**Images are recreated or blurred and labelled**

Adequate evidence: No real data or premises shown; recreations labelled.

- 0 — Real screenshots or photographs used.
- 1 — Altered without labelling.
- 2 — Recreated or blurred and labelled.
- 3 — As adequate, and the label says what was changed.

If below 2: Recreate the image with synthetic data and label it plainly. Show at recheck: The images.

**Uncovered material is described rather than shown**

Adequate evidence: A description standing in for unpublishable evidence.

- 0 — Published anyway.
- 1 — Omitted silently.
- 2 — Described, with the reason.
- 3 — As adequate, and the description still supports the claim.

If below 2: Write a sentence describing the evidence and why it is not shown. Show at recheck: The description.

### Portfolio contribution

Handling participant material correctly is itself portfolio evidence, and the note explaining your decisions is worth keeping visible.

## Lesson 7: Showing the work, and what an image cannot prove

Stable ID: m19-l07-v1. Core. Areas 17. Optional effort ~115 min.

**Objective.** Choose and caption the images for all three case studies so each one carries an argument rather than decorating the page.

**Bring forward.** Your artefacts, sketches, screens and records.

Images are the fastest way to communicate and the easiest way to imply something you have not shown.

### Learn

The test for an image is whether the case study loses an argument without it. A flow that shows the failure branches earns its place; a grid of screens because a page looked empty does not. The caption states the argument, so an image whose caption you cannot write is an image to cut.

Before-and-after pairs carry more than any single image because they show change under evidence. Two versions of the status wording with the test result between them demonstrate the whole loop in one glance, which is exactly the claim most portfolios assert in prose and never show.

Rough work belongs in. Paper sketches, the messy synthesis wall, the crossed-out flow — these establish that the polished thing came from somewhere. A portfolio containing only finished screens invites the suspicion that the process described never happened.

The honesty limit on images is that a screenshot shows a state, not a behaviour. It cannot show that the keyboard path worked, that the error recovered, or that anyone succeeded. Where the claim is about behaviour, the evidence is the test record and the caption should say so.

**Common misconception.** “More images make the case study more convincing.” Unargued images make it longer. One before-and-after pair with a caption that names the test result does more than twelve screens.

### Worked example

Project three keeps six images: the paper flow with the failure branches; a photograph of the synthesis cards, recreated because the originals had a participant's name; the exception table; the status page before and after the wording repair, captioned with the re-test result including the person who still read it as a promise; and the handover card. Twelve other images were cut, including four screens that showed nothing the flow did not. Every caption states an argument, and every image has alternative text written to carry the same information.

### Practice and pause points

- Select (~25 min): List candidate images and the argument each makes. Cut any image whose argument you cannot state.
- Build the pairs (~25 min): Assemble at least one before-and-after pair. Caption it with the change, the prediction and the result.
- Include the rough work (~20 min): Add sketches or synthesis material. Recreate anything that shows participant detail.
- Write the captions (~25 min): State what each image argues, not what it depicts. Say where a claim rests on a test record rather than the image.
- Write alternative text (~20 min): Write alternative text carrying the same information as the image. Check that a reader who cannot see it still follows the argument.

Pause after any step; save the artifact and next action.

**Free tool path.** A phone camera for paper work and your existing editor for recreations. No stock imagery, mockup generator or paid template is needed.

### Output

A chosen image set with a stated reason for each; At least one before-and-after pair with the test result; Rough work included alongside finished work; Alternative text for every image

### Checks

- What is the test for including an image? Answer: Whether the case study loses an argument without it. If you cannot write a caption stating the argument, cut the image.
- Why include rough work? Answer: Because only-finished work invites the suspicion that the described process did not happen. Sketches show the polish came from somewhere.
- What can a screenshot not prove? Answer: Behaviour. It shows a state existed, not that anyone succeeded, that recovery worked, or that the keyboard path was usable.

### Rubric and remediation

**Every image has a stated argument**

Adequate evidence: A caption per image naming what it argues.

- 0 — Uncaptioned or decorative images.
- 1 — Captions describing the image.
- 2 — Captions stating an argument.
- 3 — As adequate, and images without an argument were cut.

If below 2: Write the argument for each image and remove the ones that have none. Show at recheck: The captions.

**At least one before-and-after pair carries a test result**

Adequate evidence: Two versions with the change, prediction and outcome.

- 0 — No pair.
- 1 — A pair without the result.
- 2 — A pair with the result.
- 3 — As adequate, and the result includes a partial or failed repair.

If below 2: Find a repair from your records and build the pair around it. Show at recheck: The pair.

**Rough work appears alongside finished work**

Adequate evidence: Sketches or synthesis material in the case studies.

- 0 — Finished screens only.
- 1 — One rough artefact.
- 2 — Rough work in each case study that has it.
- 3 — As adequate, and participant detail was recreated rather than blurred badly.

If below 2: Photograph the paper work and include it. Show at recheck: The image set.

**Alternative text carries the same information**

Adequate evidence: Alternative text per image, tested by reading without it.

- 0 — Missing or filename text.
- 1 — Present but uninformative.
- 2 — Carries the same information.
- 3 — As adequate, and the argument survives with images removed.

If below 2: Rewrite the alternative text so the argument survives without the image. Show at recheck: The alternative text.

### Portfolio contribution

A captioned image set where every picture argues something is the difference between a case study and a gallery.

Purpose: Sets the standard for any chart or diagram shown in a case study. Effort: 45–60 selected min. Free reading, no account; published 19 May 2022. Verified 2026-09-06. ## Lesson 8: Writing the outcome when there is no outcome

Stable ID: m19-l08-v1. Core. Areas 17. Optional effort ~110 min.

**Objective.** Write an honest outcome section for each case study, using counts where you have them and saying plainly where you have none.

**Bring forward.** Your measurement records and limitations pages.

The outcome section is where portfolios most often stop being true, because it is the section everyone feels they must fill.

### Learn

Where you have counts, give them with the period and the conditions, exactly as m18 required. Eleven calls in five days becoming four in five days, with the public holiday named, is a real outcome and reads as one because of the detail rather than despite it.

Where you measured nothing, one plain sentence closes the section: the design was not deployed and no measurement was taken. A reviewer reads that as accuracy. What they read as inaccuracy is a paragraph of hedged language implying an improvement that was never observed.

The percentage temptation is strongest here and should be refused hardest. A rate derived from single-digit counts looks like a business result and is a claim you would have to withdraw the moment anyone asked how many people that was.

Process outcomes count. “The test showed two of three people reading the status as a promise, so the wording changed and one of two re-testers still did” is an outcome about the work. It is smaller than a business result and it is true, which is the trade this whole course makes.

**Common misconception.** “Without a measurable result the project looks like a failure.” Without an honest result the whole portfolio looks unreliable. A project with no measurement, plainly stated, costs you one sentence; a fabricated result costs you the interview.

### Worked example

Three outcome sections. Project three: calls about progress fell from eleven over five working days to four over the following five, one of which was a public holiday; the owner continued using the page for a month. Project two: not deployed; the outcome is the repair loop — two of three testers failed the task before the change, one of three after, on the same task with different people. Project one: no measurement of any kind, stated in one sentence, with the note that the accessibility repair was verified only by my own keyboard check and never tested with anyone who uses a screen reader.

### Practice and pause points

- Gather what exists (~25 min): Collect every measurement from the three projects. Note the period and conditions for each.
- Write the measured outcomes (~25 min): Give counts with periods. Name the confounds in the same sentence or the next.
- Write the unmeasured ones (~20 min): State plainly that nothing was measured. Give the process outcome instead, if there is one.
- Remove the inflation (~20 min): Search for percentages, “significantly”, “dramatically” and “improved”. Replace each with what was actually observed.
- Read as a sceptic (~20 min): Ask of each sentence: how many people, over what period? Rewrite anything that cannot answer.

Pause after any step; save the artifact and next action.

**Free tool path.** Your existing records and plain writing.

### Output

An outcome section per case study; Counts with periods and conditions where measured; A plain sentence where nothing was measured; No percentages derived from small counts

### Checks

- What goes in the outcome section with no measurement? Answer: One plain sentence saying nothing was measured, plus the process outcome if there is one. Hedged implication is worse than the plain sentence.
- Why refuse percentages here? Answer: Because a rate from single-digit counts looks like a business result and collapses the moment someone asks how many people it represents.
- Is a process outcome a real outcome? Answer: Yes. A test result that changed a design and a re-test that partly worked is a true outcome about the work, which is what these projects can honestly offer.

### Rubric and remediation

**Measured outcomes are counts with periods and conditions**

Adequate evidence: Counts, periods and confounds stated together.

- 0 — Vague improvement language.
- 1 — Counts without periods or conditions.
- 2 — Counts with both.
- 3 — As adequate, and a confound is named that weakens the result.

If below 2: Restore the underlying counts and add the period and confounds. Show at recheck: The outcome sections.

**Unmeasured projects say so plainly**

Adequate evidence: A direct sentence stating no measurement was taken.

- 0 — Implied improvement.
- 1 — Hedged admission.
- 2 — Plain statement.
- 3 — As adequate, and a process outcome is given instead.

If below 2: Replace the hedging with one sentence saying nothing was measured. Show at recheck: The unmeasured sections.

**No percentages are derived from small counts**

Adequate evidence: Counts throughout, no rates.

- 0 — Percentages present.
- 1 — One rate remaining.
- 2 — Counts only.
- 3 — As adequate, and the reason for using counts is stated once.

If below 2: Convert every rate back to the counts behind it. Show at recheck: The numbers.

**No unobserved business impact is claimed**

Adequate evidence: No revenue, retention or efficiency claim without observation.

- 0 — Business impact asserted.
- 1 — Implied through adjectives.
- 2 — Only observed effects claimed.
- 3 — As adequate, and what would be needed to establish impact is named.

If below 2: Delete any claim you did not observe and say what would establish it. Show at recheck: The claims.

### Portfolio contribution

An honest outcome section is the part of a junior portfolio most likely to be tested in an interview, and the part most likely to fail if invented.

### Assigned resources

- R63: [UK Analysis Function: data visualisation charts](https://analysisfunction.civilservice.gov.uk/policy-store/data-visualisation-charts/) — Rules for honest axes, gridlines and direct labelling. Purpose: Sets the drawing rules for any chart made from these counts. Effort: 45–60 selected min. Free reading, no account; published 19 May 2022. Verified 2026-09-06. It tells you how to draw a chart honestly, not which analysis to run. Fallback: R29.
- R59: [GOV.UK: sharing user research findings](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/sharing-user-research-findings) — Headline, essential facts and supporting evidence. Internal findings context; a portfolio audience is external and less forgiving. Fallback: R04.

## Lesson 9: Making the portfolio itself usable

Stable ID: m19-l09-v1. Core. Areas 17. Optional effort ~115 min.

**Objective.** Build and check the portfolio as a designed artefact: readable on a phone, navigable by keyboard, and honest about what you checked.

**Bring forward.** Your three case studies and your accessibility practice.

The portfolio is the one interface a reviewer will definitely use, and it is assessed whether or not you intended it to be.

### Learn

A reviewer forms an opinion from the page before reading the argument. A portfolio with unreadable line lengths, text over images and no visible focus contradicts every claim inside it, and the contradiction is noticed even when it is not articulated.

Phones dominate first contact — a link opened between meetings, on a train, on a slow connection. That means the case study opening has to work in a narrow column, images must not be required to understand the argument, and the whole thing should load without waiting.

The minimum is the m13 and m14 work applied to your own site: a heading structure that makes sense read alone, a visible focus indicator, contrast that holds, alternative text on every image, and a keyboard path through the whole thing. These are the criteria you claim to apply, so failing them here is expensive.

Record what you checked and what you did not, exactly as in the projects. A line saying you ran a keyboard pass and a contrast check but have not tested with a screen reader is more credible than silence, and far more credible than an accessibility badge you cannot support.

**Common misconception.** “The portfolio site should demonstrate my visual range.” It should demonstrate that you can make something clear and usable. Range belongs inside the case studies, not in the navigation.

### Worked example

The portfolio is a single HTML file per case study plus one index, sharing one stylesheet from the design system. Checks run and recorded: heading structure read alone; keyboard path through every link with a visible focus ring; contrast on text and on the focus indicator; alternative text on all nineteen images; the whole site opened on a phone over a mobile connection with images loading last. Not checked, and stated on the about page: no screen-reader testing and no testing with anyone who uses assistive technology.

### Practice and pause points

- Build the pages (~30 min): Build an index and one page per case study. Use your existing stylesheet rather than a new visual language.
- Check the narrow screen (~25 min): Open it at phone width and read a whole case study. Fix line lengths, image sizes and anything that requires zooming.
- Run the accessibility checks (~25 min): Read the heading structure alone; tab through every link. Check contrast on text and on the focus indicator.
- Check the images (~20 min): Confirm alternative text on every image. Confirm the argument survives with images off.
- Record the checks (~15 min): Write what you checked and what you could not. Put it somewhere a reader can find.

Pause after any step; save the artifact and next action.

**Free tool path.** Plain HTML and CSS opened in a browser, as in m12. A portfolio does not require a builder, a template or a subscription.

### Output

A portfolio that reads on a narrow screen; A heading structure and keyboard path that work; Alternative text and contrast checked; A written record of what was and was not checked

### Checks

- Why does the portfolio's own craft matter so much? Answer: Because it is the one interface the reviewer definitely uses, and a page that contradicts your claims undermines them before they are read.
- Why design for the phone first? Answer: Because first contact is usually a link opened on a phone between other things, often on a slow connection.
- Why record what you did not check? Answer: Because it is the same discipline the case studies claim, and an unsupported accessibility claim is worse than an honest gap.

### Rubric and remediation

**The portfolio reads well on a narrow screen**

Adequate evidence: A case study read end to end at phone width.

- 0 — Requires zooming or horizontal scrolling.
- 1 — Readable with effort.
- 2 — Comfortable at phone width.
- 3 — As adequate, and it loads usefully before images arrive.

If below 2: Fix line length and image sizing, then read it on a phone again. Show at recheck: The narrow-screen read.

**Heading structure and keyboard path work**

Adequate evidence: A sensible heading outline and a full keyboard path with visible focus.

- 0 — Headings decorative, focus invisible.
- 1 — One of the two works.
- 2 — Both work.
- 3 — As adequate, and the heading outline alone conveys the argument.

If below 2: Rebuild the headings semantically and restore a visible focus style. Show at recheck: The outline and keyboard pass.

**Images have alternative text and contrast holds**

Adequate evidence: Alternative text on all images and checked contrast.

- 0 — Missing on several.
- 1 — Mostly present.
- 2 — All present and contrast checked.
- 3 — As adequate, and the argument survives with images disabled.

If below 2: Add the missing alternative text and re-check contrast. Show at recheck: The image and contrast check.

**Checks performed and skipped are recorded**

Adequate evidence: A visible note of what was and was not tested.

- 0 — No record, or an unsupported claim.
- 1 — Checks recorded, gaps omitted.
- 2 — Both recorded.
- 3 — As adequate, and it distinguishes self-checks from testing with disabled people.

If below 2: Write the list of checks and the list of gaps, and publish it. Show at recheck: The record.

### Portfolio contribution

The portfolio site is itself a case study in miniature, and the check record on it demonstrates the discipline the case studies describe.

Purpose: Sets the standard for the portfolio's own accessibility. Effort: 60–90 selected min. Free reading, no account. Verified 2026-09-06. A filterable index rather than a course; reading a criterion is not conformance, which only an implemented, tested build can approach. Fallback: R41.

## Lesson 10: Presenting one case study aloud

Stable ID: m19-l10-v1. Core. Areas 17. Optional effort ~110 min.

**Objective.** Prepare and deliver a ten-minute spoken version of your strongest case study, recorded, reviewed and delivered again.

**Bring forward.** Your strongest case study.

You will be asked to walk someone through your work aloud, and reading the page aloud is not the same thing.

### Learn

The spoken version uses the same structure as the written one, because the structure answers the listener's questions in the order they arise. What changes is compression: ten minutes holds about five substantial points, and choosing them is most of the preparation.

Rehearsing against the clock is what stops the common failure, which is spending seven minutes on context and ninety seconds on everything you actually did. If the problem statement takes more than two minutes, it is too long.

Numbers spoken aloud drift upward. “About two thirds of people” is easier to say than “two of three people”, and it is a different claim. Say the counts as written, including the awkward ones.

Recording is uncomfortable and irreplaceable. You will hear the filler, the places you rush, the claim you softened, and the point where you started explaining the interface instead of the decision. Watch it once, note three changes, and deliver again.

**Common misconception.** “I will just talk through the slides.” Slides read aloud are slower than reading and less clear than either. Prepare the spoken version as its own artefact with its own five points.

### Worked example

Ten minutes, five points: the shop owner's problem with the call count; five participants and the contradiction between speed and certainty; the decision to build a status page and the not-building list; the wording repair, its prediction, and the person who still read it as a promise; and the limits, including that nobody who stopped using the shop was reached. First recording ran fourteen minutes, spent four on context and said “about two thirds” once. Second recording ran ten and a half, opened with the count, and used the exact numbers.

### Practice and pause points

- Choose five points (~25 min): Write the five things worth ten minutes. Cut anything that is context rather than evidence or decision.
- Prepare the opening (~20 min): Open with the problem and a count, in under two minutes. Say your role and the project category in one sentence.
- Record once (~25 min): Deliver it without stopping and record it. Note the total time.
- Review (~20 min): Watch once and note three specific changes. Check every number you said against what you wrote.
- Deliver again (~20 min): Deliver the revised version within the time. Keep both recordings.

Pause after any step; save the artifact and next action.

**Free tool path.** A phone camera or the recorder you already have. No presentation software or meeting subscription is required.

### Output

A five-point spoken structure; A first recording with a timing; Three specific changes identified; A second recording within the time

### Checks

- How much fits in ten minutes? Answer: About five substantial points. Choosing them, rather than compressing everything, is most of the preparation.
- Why record it? Answer: Because you will hear the filler, the rushed section, the softened claim and the drift into describing the interface instead of the decision.
- Why say counts rather than fractions? Answer: Because “about two thirds” is easier to say than “two of three” and is a different, larger claim than your evidence supports.

### Rubric and remediation

**Five points chosen for ten minutes**

Adequate evidence: A written five-point structure.

- 0 — Everything included.
- 1 — Reduced but still overfull.
- 2 — Five points that fit.
- 3 — As adequate, and the cuts were context rather than evidence.

If below 2: Cut to five points and time it again. Show at recheck: The structure.

**The opening carries problem, count and role**

Adequate evidence: An opening under two minutes with all three.

- 0 — Long context first.
- 1 — Problem stated, role omitted.
- 2 — All three, briefly.
- 3 — As adequate, and a listener stopping there would understand the project.

If below 2: Rewrite the opening and time it. Show at recheck: The opening.

**Numbers spoken match what was written**

Adequate evidence: A check of spoken numbers against the case study.

- 0 — Numbers inflated or rounded up.
- 1 — One drift.
- 2 — All match.
- 3 — As adequate, and the awkward counts were said as they are.

If below 2: List the numbers you will say and rehearse them exactly. Show at recheck: The recording.

**Two recordings exist with changes between them**

Adequate evidence: Two recordings and three identified changes.

- 0 — One recording, or none.
- 1 — Two recordings, no analysis.
- 2 — Two with three changes identified.
- 3 — As adequate, and the second is within the time.

If below 2: Watch the first, note three changes, and record again. Show at recheck: The second recording.

### Portfolio contribution

A recorded walkthrough is portfolio evidence in its own right and preparation for the conversation that decides the outcome.

## Lesson 11: Answering the questions you would rather avoid

Stable ID: m19-l11-v1. Core. Areas 17. Optional effort ~115 min.

**Objective.** Prepare honest answers to the hardest questions your portfolio invites, and practise saying what you do not know.

**Bring forward.** Your case studies, limits and audit table.

The questions that decide the outcome are the ones aimed at your weakest evidence, and they are predictable.

### Learn

The hard questions are already written in your limitations pages. If you reached nobody who stopped using the shop, someone will ask what those people think. If nothing was measured on project one, someone will ask how you know it helped. Reading your own limits as a question list takes twenty minutes and removes most of the surprise.

The correct shape of an answer is concession first, then reasoning, then next step. “I did not reach them, which means I cannot say whether the problem looks the same from their side; the smallest thing that would tell me is four conversations with people who left.” That answer is stronger than any defence, because it demonstrates the judgement the question was testing.

Not knowing is allowed. What is not allowed is filling the silence with an invention, and the pressure to do so is real: the question implies you should know, and a plausible sentence is always available. This is the moment the whole course's discipline is actually tested.

Practise aloud, because written answers do not transfer. Say the concession sentence until it arrives without hesitation, since hesitation is what pushes people into the plausible invention.

**Common misconception.** “A good answer defends the work.” A good answer tells the truth about the work. Interviewers are testing calibration more than results, and a well-defended overclaim fails that test completely.

### Worked example

Twelve questions were drawn from the three limitations pages, including: what do customers who never came back think; how do you know the page was used at all; was the fall in calls just a quiet week; why did you not test with a screen-reader user; and what would you do differently with a month. Each got a written answer conceding first. Two answers were “I do not know”, each with the smallest next step attached. All twelve were practised aloud; the quiet-week question was the one that produced hesitation, so it was rehearsed until the concession came first.

### Practice and pause points

- Mine your limits (~25 min): Turn each limitation into the question it invites. Add the questions you hope nobody asks.
- Write the answers (~30 min): Concede first, then reason, then give the next step. Keep each answer under a minute.
- Find the ones you do not know (~20 min): Mark the questions you genuinely cannot answer. Attach the smallest next step to each.
- Practise aloud (~25 min): Say each answer aloud until the concession comes first. Note where you hesitate and rehearse those again.
- Have someone ask them (~15 min): Ask someone to put the questions to you unprompted. Record any answer that drifted from what you wrote.

Pause after any step; save the artifact and next action.

**Free tool path.** Your own records, writing and speaking aloud.

### Output

A question list drawn from your own limits; Written answers that concede before reasoning; At least one honest “I do not know” with a next step; The answers practised aloud

### Checks

- Where do the hard questions come from? Answer: Your own limitations pages. Reading them as a question list removes most of the surprise in twenty minutes.
- What shape should an answer take? Answer: Concession, then reasoning, then the smallest next step. That demonstrates the judgement the question is testing.
- What is the real risk under pressure? Answer: Filling a silence with a plausible invention. A rehearsed “I do not know, and here is what would tell me” prevents it.

### Rubric and remediation

**Questions are drawn from your own limits**

Adequate evidence: A question list traceable to the limitations pages.

- 0 — Generic interview questions.
- 1 — Some drawn from limits.
- 2 — All drawn from your own limits.
- 3 — As adequate, and it includes the questions you hoped to avoid.

If below 2: Turn each line of the limitations pages into a question. Show at recheck: The question list.

**Answers concede before reasoning**

Adequate evidence: Answers whose first clause is the concession.

- 0 — Defensive answers.
- 1 — Concession buried mid-answer.
- 2 — Concession first throughout.
- 3 — As adequate, and each ends with a next step.

If below 2: Rewrite each answer to lead with what you cannot claim. Show at recheck: The answers.

**At least one honest unknown with a next step**

Adequate evidence: A question answered “I do not know” plus an action.

- 1 — An unknown admitted without a next step.
- 2 — Admitted with the smallest next step.
- 3 — As adequate, and the next step is proportionate and specific.

## Lesson 12: Final check and publishing

Stable ID: m19-l12-v1. Core. Areas 17. Optional effort ~120 min.

**Objective.** Check the finished portfolio against its three promises and every honesty rule this course holds, then publish it deliberately.

**Bring forward.** Everything from this module.

The last check is the only thing standing between an honest portfolio and one that quietly overclaims.

### Learn

The promise check is mechanical: read the promise, read the case study, and decide whether a stranger would agree it was kept. A promise that is not kept means either the case study changes or the promise does, and the second option is usually right.

Claims travel between pages. A number softened in one case study can appear inflated on the index page, and an about page written last is where unsupported summaries collect. Sweep the whole thing at once, including navigation, headings and the about page.

Hosting is a choice with the same properties as a resource: it has a cost, an account requirement and terms. Whatever you choose, check it against this course's rules — free without a card, no trial that expires into a charge — and record what you verified and when, because the answer changes over time.

A cold reader is the last useful instrument. Ask someone who does not know the projects to read it and tell you what you can do; the gap between their answer and your three claims is the portfolio's actual message, and it is usually not the one you intended.

**Common misconception.** “It is finished when it looks finished.” It is finished when the promises are kept, the claims are supported, and a cold reader arrives at the message you intended.

### Worked example

The check produced eleven changes. Two promises were kept as written; one was rewritten, because the second case study promised to show a repair loop and actually showed a single change with no re-test, so the promise now says that. The claim sweep found three unsupported sentences on the index page and one on the about page, all softened. The hosting option was checked against the course's rules on the day of publishing and the verification date recorded. A cold reader said the portfolio showed someone who tests things and admits what did not work, which matched two claims of three; the third was strengthened in the index.

### Practice and pause points

- Check the promises (~25 min): Read each promise, then its case study. Rewrite the promise or the case study where they disagree.
- Sweep the claims (~30 min): Read every page including index, about and navigation. Soften or cut anything the artefacts do not support.
- Verify the hosting (~20 min): Check cost, account and terms against this course's rules. Record what you verified and the date.
- Cold read (~25 min): Ask someone unfamiliar to read it and say what you can do. Compare their answer with your three claims.
- Publish and record (~20 min): Publish deliberately and note the date and what was included. Keep the version you published.

Pause after any step; save the artifact and next action.

**Free tool path.** The portfolio files, one conversation and a checklist. If a host requires a card or a trial that becomes a charge, choose another route or share the files directly.

### Output

A promise check per case study, with any promise rewritten; A whole-portfolio claim sweep including the index and about pages; A verified, recorded hosting decision; A cold reader's account of what the portfolio says

### Checks

- What happens when a promise is not kept? Answer: Either the case study changes or the promise does, and rewriting the promise to match the evidence is usually the right move.
- Why sweep claims across the whole portfolio? Answer: Because index and about pages are written last and collect unsupported summaries, even when each case study is careful.
- Why treat hosting like a resource? Answer: Because it has a cost, an account requirement and terms that change. Verify it and record the date, as with any other source.

### Rubric and remediation

**Each promise is checked and reconciled**

Adequate evidence: A promise-by-promise check with outcomes.

- 0 — Not checked.
- 1 — Checked informally.
- 2 — Checked with disagreements reconciled.
- 3 — As adequate, and at least one promise was rewritten to match the evidence.

If below 2: Read each promise beside its case study and reconcile the two. Show at recheck: The promise check.

**The claim sweep covers every page**

Adequate evidence: A sweep including index, about and navigation, with changes made.

- 0 — Case studies only.
- 1 — Most pages.
- 2 — Every page swept.
- 3 — As adequate, and unsupported summaries were found and cut.

If below 2: Read the index and about pages against the artefacts. Show at recheck: The sweep record.

**The hosting decision is verified and recorded**

Adequate evidence: A dated note of cost, account requirement and terms.

- 0 — Unverified.
- 1 — Checked without recording.
- 2 — Verified and dated.
- 3 — As adequate, and an option requiring a card was rejected.

If below 2: Check the terms today and write down what you found and when. Show at recheck: The hosting note.

**A cold reader's account is compared with the claims**

Adequate evidence: What an unfamiliar reader says the portfolio shows, beside your claims.

- 0 — No cold read.
- 1 — Read by someone who knows the projects.
- 2 — Cold read and compared.
- 3 — As adequate, and the comparison changed something.

If below 2: Ask someone unfamiliar what the portfolio says you can do. Show at recheck: The comparison.
